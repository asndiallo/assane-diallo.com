## What drivers see when they search

> **Do this**
>
> - Search "auto repair" and your city on your phone, then one of your main repairs, like "brake repair".

Search "auto repair" with the name of your city, on your phone. On the searches I checked, Google showed a map with three shops first, sometimes after an ad. Each one showed the rating, the number of reviews, years in business, the type of shop, the hours, the phone number, and one line from a review.

The good news for an independent shop: in plumbing or air conditioning, the top names often have thousands of reviews. Here, a shop with a hundred honest reviews and a complete profile is in the running.

Google says it ranks the free listings on relevance (how well your profile matches the search), distance, and prominence (how well known you are, including reviews and links to your site). You can't pay for a better place in them.[^rank]

> **What I saw**
>
> The shops in the "auto repair" list had **64, 111 and 121 reviews**. Their review lines were about trust and speed: a "reliable mechanic", a car "repaired within an hour".
>
> For specific repairs, the page changed. "Brake repair near me" brought a block of paid listings first, then a map list where the shops used the narrower "Brake shop" category and the review lines named the job: "front and rear brake job", "new brake line within 20 minutes".

## Fix these five things first

> **Do this**
>
> - Claim and verify your Google Business Profile. If a former partner or an old marketing company controls it, request ownership.
> - Make your hours right every day, including Saturdays and holidays.
> - Make sure the phone gets answered during those hours.
> - List your repairs and the makes you work on.
> - Upload real photos: the outside of the shop and the sign, the bays, your team at work.

A driver with a dead car calls the next shop. The [free Google checklist](/google-checklist/) goes through the rest, starting with what matters most.

## List the repairs you do, and the cars you work on

> **Do this**
>
> - Add your services one by one in your profile's services list, with a short description and a starting price where you can.
> - Say which makes you work on, and which you don't.
> - Use drivers' words, not only trade terms.

So tell Google, and your website, exactly what you fix:[^services]

- **Your services,** for example: "Check-engine light diagnosis", "Brake pads and rotors", "A/C recharge and leak repair", "Transmission service", "State inspection" (only if you're a licensed station).
- **The makes:** "We work on Toyota, Honda, Ford and Chevy; no European cars" saves everyone a phone call.
- **Drivers' words:** "check engine light", "car won't start", "AC blowing warm".

> **What I saw**
>
> When I asked ChatGPT for "auto repair near me", each shop it listed came with one line on what it does: "independent mechanic, general repairs and maintenance", "full-service repairs, A/C, cooling systems", "tires, brakes, batteries, alignments". Then it asked three questions: which vehicle, what repair (check-engine light, brakes, transmission, A/C, tires, inspection…), and what matters most (affordable and trustworthy, quality, or speed). It promised to find "the best shop for the actual problem, not just the closest one".

## Category, name and years in business

> **Do this**
>
> - Pick "Auto repair shop" or "Mechanic" as your main category for a general shop, and add narrower ones only for work you really do.
> - Use your name exactly as it appears on your sign.
> - Add your opening date.

- **Main category:** narrower categories like "Brake shop" or "Tire shop" are for work you really do. Google asks for as few categories as possible, as specific as possible, and not used as keywords.[^guidelines]
- **Name:** adding "Best Cheap Brakes" to the name breaks Google's rules and can get the profile suspended.
- **Opening date:** Google may use it to show how many years you've been in business, right under your name.[^opening] For a mechanic, years in business is trust.

## Show why you can be trusted

> **Do this**
>
> - List the certifications you really hold, like ASE certification or AAA Approved status. Never claim one you don't have.
> - Explain your warranty on parts and labor in plain words: how long, and what it covers.
> - Say how you price: a written estimate before any work, a call before anything extra, the old parts shown or returned if the customer wants them.
> - Give your diagnostic fee, and whether it's applied to the repair.
> - If you run a special, say exactly what it includes.

Drivers are afraid of being overcharged, and they look for proof before they call. Put what's true on your profile and your website.

> **What I saw**
>
> The first web result under my search was a Reddit thread titled "looking for a trustworthy mechanic". "What does a $99 brake special include?" was one of the questions Google showed for brake searches.

## Hours, Google calling you, and mobile mechanics

> **Do this**
>
> - Set special hours for holidays ahead of time.
> - Answer Google's automated calls like a customer, or turn them off in your profile settings.
> - If you're a mobile mechanic, hide your address and set a service area instead.

**Holidays:** special hours tell drivers you're open, or not, before they come.[^special]

**Google may call you for a customer.** Google can call or text shops to ask about prices and availability for the customer. The caller says it's automated. Answer it like a customer. A verified profile can turn these calls off in Business Profile settings, under "Google automated calls and text messages".[^calls]

**Mobile mechanics:** if customers don't come to you, Google wants you to hide the address and set a service area instead (up to 20 cities or ZIP codes, within about two hours' drive). If you have a shop and also do roadside or at-home work, you can show both.[^areas]

> **What I saw**
>
> For "auto repair", Google showed a "Have AI get prices" button. A mobile brake service with 293 reviews and no address sat in the map list I saw.

## Photos

> **Do this**
>
> - Photograph the front of the shop and your sign, from the street.
> - Photograph clean bays, your equipment, and your team at work.
> - Take before and after photos when a repair shows: a dented panel, worn pads next to new ones.
> - Use only your own photos, never stock images.

Google asks for photos that are in focus, well lit, and not heavily filtered, and a photo of the outside helps people find you.[^photos]

## Reviews: ask after every job

> **Do this**
>
> - Text your Google review link when the customer picks up the car, or put a card with a QR code on the counter.
> - Ask every customer.
> - Reply to every review: short, personal, about the car and the job.

In BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults, about local businesses in general, 74% look for reviews from the last three months, 68% only choose businesses with four stars or more, and 89% expect the owner to reply.[^brightlocal]

**What not to do.** Two sets of rules apply:

- Google's rules forbid offering anything in exchange for a review, like a discount on the next oil change, and asking only the customers you know are happy.[^policy]
- The FTC's rule on reviews, in effect since October 2024, bans fake reviews (including ones written by AI), paying for positive reviews, undisclosed reviews from employees, and threats to get a bad review removed. It also limits owners and managers asking their relatives for reviews. Courts can fine businesses that break it knowingly.[^ftc]

Never write reviews for customers. Their own words are what Google shows: a review that says "front and rear brake job, car runs great" is the line the next driver reads.

**How to reply.** For a bad review: thank them, explain what happened without blaming, say what you'll do, and offer to talk. Never share a customer's details.

## Beyond Google: Yelp, Apple Maps, AI assistants and neighbors

> **Do this**
>
> - Claim your Yelp page and keep it the same as Google.
> - Claim your listing on Apple Business Connect.
> - If you qualify, get listed in directories that check shops, like AAA's list of approved repair facilities.

- **Yelp:** Yelp says ChatGPT shows its reviews, ratings, photos and business details.[^yelp]
- **Apple Maps** is the maps app on every iPhone. Apple Business Connect is free and lets you control your listing.[^apple]
- **Neighbors:** you can't make anyone recommend you, and you should never post fake recommendations; honest work gets mentioned.
- **AI answers:** in BrightLocal's survey, 45% had used ChatGPT or another AI tool to find a local business.[^brightlocal] Nobody can promise what an AI will say about you; make sure everything it reads is accurate, complete and the same everywhere.

> **What I saw**
>
> Yelp's "Best 10" list, AAA's list of approved repair facilities, and Reddit and Facebook threads asking for "a trustworthy mechanic" all ranked on the first page. ChatGPT also shows ads now: under my answer sat one from a tools retailer.

## Your website, kept simple

> **Do this**
>
> - Put a phone number people can tap to call at the top of every page.
> - List your repairs one by one, and the makes you work on.
> - Show your hours, address and a way to book or ask for a quote.
> - Explain your warranty, how estimates work, and your diagnostic fee.
> - List the certifications you really hold.
> - Put your trade and city in the page title, for example "Auto repair in San Antonio".

You don't need a fancy site. You need one that answers what a worried driver asks, on a phone.

## What takes an afternoon, and what takes months

In an afternoon: claim your profile, fix hours, list your repairs and makes, add the opening date, upload real photos, and claim Yelp and Apple Business Connect.

Over the following months: a review after every job, a reply to each one, new photos, and being mentioned where drivers ask for a mechanic. Google takes time to reflect this. It usually takes months, and nobody can honestly guarantee where you'll rank.
