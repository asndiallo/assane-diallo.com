## What people see when they search

> **Do this**
>
> - Search your trade and your city on your phone, and scroll down to where the free listings start.

Search "plumber" or "AC repair" with the name of your city, on your phone. Before any free listing, you'll usually scroll past two kinds of ads:

- **A block of paid listings** with a rating, the number of reviews, years in business, the area served, "Open 24 hours", and buttons to get a quote, book online or get the phone number. These are Google's Local Services Ads.
- **Text ads** that look like regular results with "Sponsored" above them.

Then comes the map with three businesses, the free listings. Each shows the rating, the number of reviews, years in business, the city, the hours, the phone number, and usually one line from a review.

Google says it ranks the free listings on three things: relevance (how well your profile matches the search), distance, and prominence (how well known you are, which includes your reviews and the websites that link to you). You can't pay for a better place in them.[^rank] The big companies in your area may have thousands of reviews, but they don't own the list.

> **What I saw**
>
> On the searches I checked, the review lines were almost all about speed: fixed "in under an hour", help "the same day". On one AC repair search, a company with 138 reviews sat right next to companies with 582 and 4,400.

## Fix these five things first

> **Do this**
>
> - Claim and verify your Google Business Profile. If a former partner or an old marketing company controls it, request ownership.
> - Set your service area the right way (next section).
> - Make sure the phone number on your profile gets answered.
> - List your services one by one.
> - Upload real photos: your truck, your team, and your work.

Owning the profile comes first: everything else depends on it. In this trade, a missed call is usually a lost job: people call the next name on the list.

The [free Google checklist](/google-checklist/) goes through the rest, starting with what matters most.

## Your service area: where you really go

> **Do this**
>
> - If customers don't come to your address, hide it and set a service area instead.
> - List only the cities or ZIP codes you really cover, up to 20, within about two hours' drive.
> - If customers also come to you, show the address and the service area together.

If customers don't come to your address, Google wants you to hide it and show a service area instead. Its own example is a plumber who runs the business from home: clear the address from the profile.[^guidelines]

- You can list up to 20 cities or ZIP codes. You can't draw a circle around your address.[^areas]
- Google says the whole area shouldn't reach farther than about two hours of driving from where you're based.[^guidelines]
- A service area stretched to look bigger brings calls you'll turn down, and those callers don't come back.
- A showroom or a counter where customers come counts as an address. A mailbox, a virtual office or a shared desk without your sign and staff doesn't count as an address on Google.

## List every service you offer

> **Do this**
>
> - Add each service from Google's suggestions for your trade, and your own where they're missing.
> - Give each service a short description and, if you can, a starting price.
> - Use the words customers use, and say "home" when it matters.
> - Group services if you do several trades.

Google offers a list of services suggested for your trade (its plumbing example is "Install faucet"), and you can add your own.[^services]

- **Customers' words:** "water heater installation", "AC not cooling", "drain cleaning", "move-out cleaning".
- **Who you serve:** write "home AC repair", "central air" or "HVAC", and say who you serve (houses, apartments, businesses), so nobody has to guess.
- **Several trades:** group them (plumbing, heating, cooling).
- **Names:** don't put prices or phone numbers in a service's name: Google rejects them.

> **What I saw**
>
> When I searched "emergency plumber near me", Google showed a single plumber's panel instead of a map, and the panel listed that company's services one by one: repiping, slab leak repair, water line repair, drain cleaning. Those came straight from the profile. When I asked ChatGPT for "AC repair near me", it assumed I meant a car and listed auto shops.

## Name, category and years in business

> **Do this**
>
> - Use your name exactly as it appears on your truck, your invoices and your website.
> - Pick the most specific main category for your main trade, and add others only for trades you really do.
> - Add your opening date.

- **Your name:** adding words like "24/7 Emergency Plumber Best Prices" to the name breaks Google's rules and can get the profile suspended.[^guidelines]
- **Your main category:** for cleaning or handyman work, pick the closest one the dashboard offers.
- **Your opening date:** Google may use it to show how many years you've been in business, which appears right under your name in the results.[^opening]

> **What I saw**
>
> On the searches I checked, businesses used "Plumber", "HVAC contractor" and the narrower "Air conditioning repair service".

## Hours, emergencies, and Google calling you

> **Do this**
>
> - Show "Open 24 hours" only if someone answers at 2 a.m.
> - If you take emergency calls only for some jobs, say so in your description and on your website.
> - Set special hours for holidays ahead of time, and confirm them even when they don't change.
> - Answer Google's automated calls like a customer, or turn them off in your profile settings.
> - Text missed callers right away, so they know you'll call back.

"Open 24 hours" sits right next to competitors who claim it, so a wrong one is noticed fast, usually in a review. Google suggests confirming your hours on public holidays even when they don't change.[^special]

**Google may call you on a customer's behalf.** On some searches, Google now offers to "get prices" or "check appointments" for the customer, then calls or texts businesses to ask about prices and availability. The caller says it's automated. Answer it like a customer: it is one. If you don't want these calls, a verified profile can turn them off in Business Profile settings, under "Google automated calls and text messages". (Not available in Indiana, Louisiana, Minnesota, Montana and Nebraska.)[^calls]

## Photos of real jobs

> **Do this**
>
> - Photograph your truck or van, with your name on it, and your team if they're happy to be in it.
> - Take before and after photos: the leak, the new water heater, the clean kitchen.
> - Add a few after each week of work, and never use stock images.

Take one job per photo, on your phone, in good light. People want to see who will come to their house. Google asks for photos that are in focus, well lit, and not heavily filtered.[^photos]

## Reviews: ask after every job

> **Do this**
>
> - Text your Google review link the same day, while the fix is fresh.
> - Leave a card with a QR code on the counter.
> - Ask every customer, not only the happy ones.
> - Reply to every review: short, personal, about the job.

In BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults, about local businesses in general:[^brightlocal]

- 74% look for reviews from the last three months;
- 68% only choose businesses with four stars or more;
- 47% won't choose a business with fewer than 20 reviews;
- 89% expect the owner to reply, and half are put off by generic copy-and-paste replies.

**How to ask.** A text like: "Thanks for having us today. If we did a good job, a short review helps a small business like ours a lot."

**What not to do.** No discount on the next visit in exchange for a review, and no asking only the customers you know are pleased: Google's rules forbid both, and the reviews can be removed.[^policy] Never write reviews for customers. Their own words are what Google shows: a review that says "fixed our AC the same day" is the line the next person reads.

**The law says so too.** The FTC's rule on reviews, in effect since October 2024, bans fake reviews (including ones written by AI), paying for positive reviews, undisclosed reviews from people who work for you, and threats to get a bad review removed. It also limits owners and managers asking their relatives for reviews. Courts can fine businesses that break it knowingly.[^ftc]

**How to reply.** For a bad review: thank them, own what went wrong, say what you'll do, and offer to call them. Never share a customer's address or details.

## Fake competitors

> **Do this**
>
> - Report listings that clearly break Google's rules: on the listing, choose "Suggest an edit", then "Place is closed or not here", then "Offensive, harmful, or misleading".
> - For misleading names, phone numbers or websites, use Google's Business Redressal Complaint form.

Some listings in your area may break Google's rules: names stuffed with keywords, addresses that are empty lots or mailboxes, one company under several names.[^report] Report what's clearly against the rules, not a competitor you simply don't like.

## Paid listings, and why your free profile still matters

> **Do this**
>
> - Before deciding on ads, make your free profile complete: the ads fill in from it.

The paid block at the top is Local Services Ads: you pay per lead (a call or a message), not per click. Google is moving these ads into Google Ads in stages from August 2026,[^lsa] and advertisers who pass Google's screening now show a single "Google Verified" badge. The old money-back guarantee has ended.[^verified]

Whether ads are worth it depends on your margins and how many jobs you can take; this guide doesn't push you either way. Two facts help the decision: the ads fill in from your Business Profile (name, address, hours), and the free listings sit right under them. A complete profile helps both.

## Beyond Google: Yelp, Apple Maps, AI assistants and neighbors

> **Do this**
>
> - Claim your Yelp, Thumbtack and Angi pages, keep them the same as Google, and reply to reviews there too.
> - Claim your listing on Apple Business Connect.
> - Tell local news about something real, like a free safety check for seniors. Never post fake recommendations.

- **Yelp, Thumbtack and Angi** pages and "Top 10" lists show up on the first page for plumbing and AC searches. Yelp says ChatGPT shows its reviews, ratings, photos and business details,[^yelp] and Angi and Thumbtack have announced apps inside ChatGPT.
- **Apple Maps** is the maps app on every iPhone. Apple Business Connect is free and lets you control your listing there.[^apple]
- **Neighbors and local news:** you can't make anyone recommend you; you can do work people mention.
- **AI answers:** in BrightLocal's survey, 45% had used ChatGPT or another AI tool to find a local business, up from 6% a year earlier.[^brightlocal] Nobody can promise what an AI will say about you. What you can do is make sure everything it reads, on Google, Yelp, Apple Maps and your website, is accurate, complete and the same everywhere.

> **What I saw**
>
> Reddit threads asking for an AC company and a local newspaper's "best HVAC companies" list both ranked on the first page.
>
> When I asked ChatGPT for an emergency plumber, each company it listed came with a line taken from that company's own website, cited as the source: "emergency calls and texts accepted around the clock", "advertises immediate emergency dispatch", even a discount offer. It then told the customer to ask three questions before hiring: can you come now, what's the emergency fee, will you give a written estimate.
>
> When I told it I meant home HVAC, it listed four companies within a couple of miles, with 956, 138, 36 and 14 reviews, and none of the big firms with thousands. It showed a general price table ("rough budgeting estimates, not quotes") and offered to compare companies on price, reputation and whether they charge a diagnostic fee. Under one answer sat an ad from a home-services marketplace: ChatGPT shows ads now.

## Your website, kept simple

> **Do this**
>
> - Put a phone number people can tap to call at the top of every page.
> - Answer what customers ask before hiring: emergency calls (and texts) and when, how fast you can usually come, your service-call or diagnostic fee, and whether you give a written estimate first.
> - Make a page for each main service, with what's included and how to get a quote.
> - Write out the cities you cover, the same as on Google.
> - Show your licence number if your state licenses your trade, and proof of insurance.
> - Put your trade and city in the page title, for example "Plumber in San Antonio".

You don't need a fancy site. You need one that answers the same questions as your profile, on a phone. Answers on your website travel with you: that's where ChatGPT found its lines. If your fee is on your website, it can compare yours. Customers check licences and insurance.

Only make a page for a city if it says something true about your work there. Copies of the same page with the city name swapped help nobody, and Google's spam policies call them doorway pages.[^doorways]

## What takes an afternoon, and what takes months

In an afternoon: claim your profile, set the service area, list your services, add the opening date, fix hours, upload real photos, and claim Yelp and Apple Business Connect.

Over the following months: a review after every job, a reply to each one, new photos, and being mentioned where your neighbors look. Google takes time to reflect this. It usually takes months, and nobody can honestly guarantee where you'll rank.
