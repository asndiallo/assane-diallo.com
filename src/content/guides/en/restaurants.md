## What people see before they pick you

> **Do this**
>
> - Search your main dish and your city on your phone, and look at what comes up before you change anything.

Search "jollof rice" with the name of your city and look at the top of the page. Google shows three restaurants first, on a map. For each one it shows the star rating, the number of reviews, a price range, the type of food, whether it's open right now, and one line that explains the match: a dish from the menu ("Menu highlight: jollof rice"), or a sentence from a review that uses the words searched.

Above that list sit filters like "Open now", "Top rated" and "Accepts reservations". Tap a restaurant and its profile opens with buttons to order, call, get directions, and tabs for the menu, photos and reviews. Many people decide right there and never visit a website.

Google says it ranks these results on three things: relevance (how well your profile matches the search), distance, and prominence (how well known you are, which includes your reviews and the websites that link to you). It also says plainly that you can't pay for a better local ranking.[^rank] Results also change with where the person searching is standing, so what you see on your own phone is not what everyone sees.

## Fix these five things first

> **Do this**
>
> - Claim and verify your Google Business Profile. If a former manager or an old agency controls it, request ownership.
> - Make your hours right for every day, with dine-in hours as your main hours.
> - Make sure the phone number on your profile gets answered during the hours you show.
> - Add a menu and an order link that work on a phone.
> - Upload real photos: the front from the street, your dishes, and the inside.

Owning the profile comes first: everything else depends on it. For restaurants, Google wants your dine-in hours as your main hours; takeout, delivery and pickup times go under "More hours".[^guidelines] Wrong hours take you out of "Open now" and send people to a locked door, which turns into bad reviews.

The [free Google checklist](/google-checklist/) goes through the rest, starting with what matters most.

## Your menu: name dishes the way people search for them

> **Do this**
>
> - Add your full menu in Google's menu editor: sections, dishes, descriptions and prices.
> - Write dish names the way customers type them, with a few words of explanation.
> - Add your own dish names and photos in the Google Maps app.
> - Put the same menu on your website, as text, not only a photo or a PDF.
> - Keep prices current.

People search by dish, so your menu is what connects their search to you.

- **Google's menu editor** is built for food and drink businesses: you can add sections, dishes, descriptions and prices. Changes take a day or two to show.[^menu]
- **Customers' words:** "Thieboudienne", "jollof rice", "egusi soup", "injera". If your menu only says "House Special No. 3", nobody will find it. A few words help new customers: "Thieboudienne (Senegalese fish and rice)".
- **Your dish names first:** Google says dish names you add come before the ones customers add.[^dishes]
- **Text, not pictures:** a menu typed on a web page is easy for Google and AI assistants to read; a photo or a PDF of the menu is harder.
- **Prices:** an old price on Google is a small surprise at the counter, and sometimes a review.

> **What I saw**
>
> When I asked ChatGPT for jollof rice nearby, the price it quoted came from a menu on a restaurant's own website.

## Photos

> **Do this**
>
> - Photograph the front door from the street, in daylight.
> - Photograph each signature dish on its own, near a window if you can.
> - Photograph the inside: tables, the counter, the space for events.
> - Add a few new photos every month, and never use stock images.

Google asks for photos that are in focus, well lit, and not heavily filtered, and notes that a photo of the outside helps people recognize you when they arrive.[^photos] People want to see the plate they'll get, so use only your own photos. Your team can be in them too, if they're happy to be.

## Hours, holidays and closing days

> **Do this**
>
> - Set special hours ahead of time for holidays and any closure of six days or fewer.
> - Confirm your hours on public holidays, even when they don't change.
> - For a longer closure, mark the restaurant as temporarily closed.
> - If you close for good or move, change it the same day on Google, Yelp, Apple Maps and your website.

Google uses special hours for closures of six days or fewer, and suggests confirming your hours on public holidays even when they don't change, so people know they're right.[^special] For renovation or travel, "temporarily closed" is the right setting.

AI assistants repeat whatever your listings still say. Old information keeps sending people somewhere, so make sure it sends them to the right place.

> **What I saw**
>
> When I asked ChatGPT for jollof rice nearby in November 2026, its "top pick" was a restaurant that its own map marked as permanently closed.

## Ordering, delivery and reservations

> **Do this**
>
> - Add your own ordering link and set it as preferred, for pickup and for delivery.
> - Hide the delivery apps you don't use.
> - If you use Square or Toast, check that online ordering is set up there.
> - If you take reservations, connect your booking tool and make sure your profile says so.
> - If you cater, list catering as a service and give it its own page on your website.

On your profile, the "Order pickup" and "Order delivery" buttons send people to an ordering link: yours, or a delivery app's. Delivery apps that say they work with you are listed automatically; you can set your own link as preferred and hide the others.[^ordering] Delivery apps take a cut of each order; your own link keeps the customer, and the money, with you.

New in 2026: Google's AI assistant in Maps (Ask Maps) can now place takeout orders in the US at restaurants that use Square or Toast.[^askmaps]

People can filter the results for "Accepts reservations", so a restaurant that takes them should say so. For catering, the page on your website should list the dishes, the number of people you can serve, and how to ask for a quote.

> **What I saw**
>
> On the profiles I checked, the restaurant's own choice showed first, marked "Preferred by business". On one profile, an ad for a delivery app appeared right under the restaurant's own buttons: without your own link, the apps fill the space.

## Choose the right category

> **Do this**
>
> - Pick the most specific main category that exists for your food.
> - Add "Caterer" only if you cater.
> - Keep your business name exactly as it appears on your sign.

Your main category tells Google what kind of restaurant you are. Google asks for as few categories as possible, as specific as possible, and not used as keywords.[^guidelines] Choose "West African restaurant" or "African restaurant" rather than just "Restaurant". There isn't always one for your exact cuisine; choose the closest.

Adding words like "Best jollof in town" to your name breaks Google's rules and can get the profile suspended.

## Reviews: ask every customer, answer every review

> **Do this**
>
> - Put a QR code that opens your Google review page on the table, the receipt or the takeout bag.
> - Ask every customer at the right moment, not only the ones who look happy.
> - Reply to every review: short, personal, and about what they said.

Reviews decide a lot. In BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults (about local businesses in general, not restaurants only):[^brightlocal]

- 74% look for reviews from the last three months;
- 68% only choose businesses with four stars or more;
- 47% won't choose a business with fewer than 20 reviews;
- 89% expect the owner to reply, and half are put off by generic copy-and-paste replies.

**How to ask.** The right moment is when you clear the plates, or when someone picks up an order.

**What not to do.** No discount or free dessert in exchange for a review, and no asking only your happy customers: Google's rules forbid both, and the reviews can be removed.[^policy] Don't write reviews for customers either. What they write in their own words is also what Google shows: a review that mentions your jollof rice is the line people see when they search for jollof rice.

**The law says so too.** The FTC's rule on reviews, in effect since October 2024, bans fake reviews (including ones written by AI), paying for positive reviews, undisclosed reviews from people who work for you, and threats to get a bad review removed. It also limits owners and managers asking their relatives for reviews. Courts can fine businesses that break it knowingly.[^ftc]

**How to reply.** No promotions in replies. For a bad review: thank them, own what went wrong, say what you'll do, and offer to talk directly. Never share a customer's private details.

## Beyond Google: Yelp, Apple Maps and ChatGPT

> **Do this**
>
> - Claim your Yelp page, keep it the same as Google, and reply to reviews there too.
> - Claim your listing on Apple Business Connect and add buttons for the menu, reservations, pickup and delivery.
> - Get listed in the directories that fit your food, and tell local news when you open, move or host an event.

More people now ask an AI assistant where to eat. In the same survey, 45% had used ChatGPT or another AI tool to find a local business, up from 6% a year earlier.[^brightlocal]

- **Yelp feeds ChatGPT.** Yelp says ChatGPT shows its reviews, ratings, photos and business details, and people can book through Yelp inside ChatGPT.[^yelp]
- **Apple Maps** is the maps app that comes with every iPhone. In BrightLocal's survey, 27% now read reviews there, up from 14% a year earlier. Apple Business Connect is free.[^apple]
- **Local news and directories get read** by AI assistants as well as people.

Nobody can promise what an AI will say about you. What you can do is make sure everything it reads is accurate, complete and the same everywhere.

> **What I saw**
>
> When I checked, ChatGPT showed one restaurant at 4.0 stars from Yelp while Google showed it at 4.9. Its answer also cited a local news story about a restaurant's opening and a directory of African restaurants.

## Your website, kept simple

> **Do this**
>
> - Put your menu on the site as text, with prices.
> - Make the phone number tap-to-call.
> - Show your address, hours and a link to order or book.
> - Put the type of food and your neighborhood in the page title.

You don't need a fancy site. You need one that answers the same questions as your profile, on a phone. A page title like "Senegalese restaurant in Harlem" tells Google and people what you are and where.

## What takes an afternoon, and what takes months

In an afternoon: claim your profile, fix hours, add the menu and your ordering link, upload real photos, and claim Yelp and Apple Business Connect.

Over the following months: a steady flow of new reviews, replies to every one, fresh photos, and mentions in local news and directories. Google takes time to reflect this. It usually takes months, and nobody can honestly guarantee where you'll rank.
